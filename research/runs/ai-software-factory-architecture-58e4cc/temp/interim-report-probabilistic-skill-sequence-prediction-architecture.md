# Interim report: probabilistic-skill-sequence-prediction-architecture

**Locus question:** What is the concrete architecture for mining an agent's trace history to probabilistically predict and auto-chain the next Skill/tool call in production, given two unreconciled corpus papers (FSM trace-automata vs. speculative pattern-tuple execution) that point different directions?
**Flavor:** convergent

## What the corpus already said

Before this pass, the corpus held two strong but unconnected halves of the answer, both already present as full-text sources: [[automata-from-agent-traces]] (Cho et al., "Automata from Agent Traces," ICML 2026 AIWILD workshop, arXiv:2608.23670) and [[act-while-thinking-accelerating-llm-agents-via-pattern-aware-speculative-tool-ex]] (Sui et al., "PASTE," arXiv:2603.18897, SJTU/Microsoft Research). Neither paper cites the other; they were produced by disjoint research communities (agent-safety/auditing vs. agent-serving-systems) and solve adjacent but distinct problems. The width corpus also holds two SOP-adjacent/survey notes — [[what-is-an-ai-agent-sop]] and [[github-yintonghuoawesome-agent-trajectory-a-collection-of-agent-trajectory-analy]] — which establish that (a) deterministic, rule-based "SOP" encodings are the production-support-industry's current answer to scenario-conditioned action sequencing (no probabilistic chaining at all), and (b) the broader trajectory-analysis literature (surveyed in the awesome-agent-trajectory list) treats "next-step prediction" and "failure prediction" as siblings of the same substrate, which is exactly the FSM paper's framing. [[github-addyosmaniagent-skills-production-grade-engineering-skills-for-ai-coding]] shows the current real-world "Skill chaining" baseline is manual and deterministic: slash commands (`/spec`→`/plan`→`/build`→`/test`→`/review`→`/ship`) that "activate automatically based on what you're doing," i.e., rule-triggered, not trace-mined or probabilistic. No corpus source, before this investigation, proposed a mechanism that is both probabilistic and grounded in production telemetry at the Skill-dispatch layer.

## What the new sources say

No new fetches were made (source_budget 6, used 0) — the directive for this locus explicitly identifies the gap as a *synthesis* gap between two sources already fully present in the vault, not a missing-evidence gap. I read both papers in full (sections 1–5 of PASTE including Algorithms 1–3; sections 1, 3.1–3.6 and the Theorems/Propositions of the FSM paper) to check whether a bridging architecture is actually licensed by the text, rather than inferring it from abstracts.

**[[automata-from-agent-traces]]** builds a *corpus-level* structural substrate: traces are tokenized into an "activity alphabet" (6–42 symbols: tool names, action tags, or command categories — note, this is the *same* coarse-grained symbol space PASTE uses for its "event signatures"), inserted into a prefix tree, then collapsed by a last-activity right congruence into a deterministic FSM with exactly |A|+1 states, built in milliseconds with *zero* hyperparameters (Theorem 3). Next-step prediction is then a simple order-1 conditional: "Given the current FSM state qt = δ*(q0, a1...at−1), we estimate P(at | qt) from transition counts" with additive smoothing (Eq. 4), yielding a 21% cross-entropy reduction over an identical unsmoothed baseline without state, and beating Agent Workflow Memory on 8/8 datasets. Crucially, the paper proves a *convergence guarantee* (Proposition 5): the extracted FSM matches the population automaton with probability ≥1−δ after N ≥ (1/p_min)·ln(r/δ) traces — concretely N≤690 traces for SWE-agent's 51 transitions — meaning the predictor's reliability is quantifiable and bounded, not just empirically observed. The paper stops at prediction of the *next activity symbol*; it never touches argument/parameter prediction or execution scheduling — "we recover the directly-follows automaton... not the generating automaton" and the entire contribution is confined to state abstraction, not runtime dispatch.

**[[act-while-thinking-accelerating-llm-agents-via-pattern-aware-speculative-tool-ex]]** builds a *runtime* dispatch mechanism that assumes next-tool prediction as a solved sub-problem and focuses on what to do with it. Its core abstraction is the Pattern Tuple `(C, T, f, p)`: C is "an order-preserving subsequence of event signatures" (control-flow context, mined via PrefixSpan with a min-support σ and confidence threshold τ — i.e., an ad hoc, hyperparameter-laden k-length window, explicitly weaker than the FSM paper's hyperparameter-free quotient construction), T is the predicted tool type, f is a symbolic "value mapping function" for late-bound argument resolution (e.g., `arg0 = SearchRes["list"][0]["url"]`), and p is the empirical confidence. At runtime (Algorithm 2) the system matches live event-signature suffixes against the pattern pool and emits probabilistic predicted invocations to a risk-aware scheduler (Algorithm 3) that partitions work into authoritative (correctness-critical, preemption-capable) vs. speculative (best-effort, confined to a slack-resource budget, instantly preempted on contention, and *promoted* to authoritative if the agent's real decision matches the running speculative job). Measured results: 48.5% reduction in average task completion time, 1.8× tool-execution throughput, with empirically observed control-flow regularities as strong as "55% of file_editor writes are immediately followed by a terminal/pytest call" and "95% of visit URLs are a strict substring of the preceding search's JSON output." The paper never proposes a principled state-abstraction for C — it reaches for PrefixSpan sequential-pattern mining instead, which is a known inferior approximation of exactly the problem the FSM paper solves formally (the FSM paper's own baseline comparison shows k-Tails-family methods, PrefixSpan's cousin, need 1.4–10× more states than the FSM's deterministic quotient for the same fitness).

## Evidence synthesis

The two papers are not in tension — the locus's framing of them as "point[ing] different directions" is itself the thing to correct. They operate at different layers of the same pipeline and were simply never composed by their respective authors, because one community (agent-safety/auditing) measures "prediction" in bits of cross-entropy over the *symbol stream*, while the other (systems/serving) measures "prediction" in milliseconds of *wall-clock latency saved* by acting on a predicted symbol before the LLM emits it. Nothing in either paper's formalism is incompatible with the other. The FSM paper's P(a|q) is a probability distribution over the *next activity symbol*, which is exactly the "target T" slot in PASTE's Pattern Tuple; PASTE's own candidate-context-mining step (PrefixSpan over k-windows) is a strictly worse version of the FSM paper's deterministic prefix-tree-plus-congruence construction, evaluated by the FSM paper's own baseline table. The genuine complementarity: the FSM paper has nothing to say about *arguments* (it only predicts tool/action identity, never parameters), while PASTE's entire operational value proposition — the 48.5%/1.8× numbers — depends on the value-mapping function f, which the FSM paper doesn't have. Each paper is solving the half of the problem the other ignores: FSM = "what's the probability distribution over next Skill, and how do you know it's reliable" (with a convergence proof); PASTE = "given a probable next Skill, how do you safely pre-execute it without corrupting state or stalling the critical path."

The one place a real design decision has to be made, and where the corpus is silent, is *which context representation feeds the predictor*. PASTE's own context-mining (PrefixSpan, hyperparameters σ/τ) is empirically inferior per the FSM paper's cross-method comparison (RPNI/k-Tails-family needs 1.4–3,036× more states for equal fitness). This is not a hypothetical substitution — the FSM paper's Eq. 4 and the PASTE paper's pattern-pool confidence score p are structurally the same object (a smoothed conditional frequency), computed over the same symbol granularity (tool/action type, 6–42 symbols vs. PASTE's "event signatures"). Replacing PASTE's context-mining step with the FSM's deterministic, hyperparameter-free, provably-convergent state construction is a drop-in substitution that inherits a formal reliability guarantee (Proposition 5) PASTE's own PrefixSpan approach lacks.

### Recommended bridging architecture for a Skill-dispatch runtime

1. **Per-Skill-category trace miner (offline/continuous, FSM-paper method).** Partition agent-trace logs by role/task-type (e.g., coding-agent, research-agent, review-agent Skill families — mirroring the factory's likely org-chart decomposition). For each partition, build/rebuild the deterministic FSM (prefix tree → last-activity congruence → rare-transition filter) incrementally; this is linear-time and sub-second per the paper's own benchmarks, so it can run as a background job triggered on every N new completed traces, not a nightly batch. Output: a versioned, auditable FSM artifact per Skill category, checked into the Skill registry alongside the Skill definitions themselves (bridges directly to [[github-addyosmaniagent-skills-production-grade-engineering-skills-for-ai-coding]]'s existing Skill-packaging convention).
2. **Runtime next-Skill predictor.** At each agent step, walk the session's activity sequence through the relevant FSM to the current state q_t, compute smoothed P(next-Skill = a | q_t) via Eq. 4. This *is* the "probabilistic sequence" Pillar 2 asks for, and it comes with a quantified reliability bound: don't activate speculative dispatch for a Skill category until N ≥ (1/p_min)·ln(r/δ) observed traces for that category's transition count r (concretely, ~700 traces per Skill-category is the right provisioning target before trusting the predictor, per the FSM paper's own worked example).
3. **Speculative dispatch layer (PASTE's scheduler, FSM-fed).** Top-k predicted Skills with P(a|q_t) ≥ τ become Pattern Tuples (C = q_t, T = predicted Skill, f = PASTE-style value-mapping function mined for that specific FSM transition, p = P(a|q_t) from Eq. 4 instead of PrefixSpan support/confidence). Dispatch via PASTE's four-stage scheduler unchanged: authoritative Skill invocations always preempt; speculative jobs run only on slack compute; a match promotes the speculative job to authoritative (result reused, zero redundant work); a miss aborts it for free.
4. **Side-effect gating (the one place neither paper covers, and the one place the factory context changes the calculus).** PASTE's own safety argument restricts speculation to contexts where mispredictions are cheap to discard. A software factory's Skills include state-mutating actions (deploy, merge, force-push, delete) where speculative *execution* is unacceptable even under preemption semantics, because some side effects (a push, a paid API call, a destructive migration) aren't cleanly abortable. The correct extension — not present in either source, and the one inferential leap in this architecture — is to split PASTE's "speculative invocation" into two tiers: (a) idempotent/read-only Skills (search, lint, test-in-sandbox, context-fetch) get full speculative *execution* exactly as PASTE describes; (b) mutating Skills get speculative *staging only* (pre-warm the container, pre-resolve arguments via f, pre-compute the diff) with the actual mutating call gated behind the authoritative decision. This preserves PASTE's measured latency wins on the read-heavy majority of Skill calls while not inheriting its rollback assumptions for the minority of Skills where rollback is actually expensive or impossible in a production software factory.
5. **Shared failure-risk brake.** The FSM paper's per-state failure-prediction features (AUROC up to 0.94) and online early-stopping monitor (triggers at 25–32% completion) should gate the speculative layer, not run alongside it independently: a trajectory flagged as elevated failure-risk should suppress speculative dispatch for that session (conserve slack compute, avoid committing to a probably-wrong chain) rather than being treated as an unrelated concern.

## Committed position

The two corpus papers are not a genuine unreconciled tension — they are a vertically stacked pair that the literature simply never composed, and the right production architecture for Pillar 2's "probabilistic sequences" ask is a straightforward layering: use the FSM paper's deterministic, hyperparameter-free, convergence-bounded state construction as the context/probability engine (replacing PASTE's weaker PrefixSpan-based context mining), and use PASTE's Pattern-Tuple abstraction plus risk-aware opportunistic scheduler as the execution/argument-resolution/dispatch engine, with one addition neither source provides: a two-tier speculation policy that permits full speculative execution only for idempotent Skills and restricts mutating Skills to speculative staging gated behind the authoritative call. The load-bearing reason this works is that both papers operate over the same coarse symbol granularity (tool/action-type identity, not natural-language content) and the same conditional-probability object (a smoothed next-symbol distribution), so the FSM's P(a|q_t) slots directly into PASTE's pattern-tuple target/probability fields without any representational mismatch — this is a composition, not a reconciliation of competing claims.

- **Position:** The production architecture for trace-driven probabilistic Skill-chaining should use FSM state-conditioning (Cho et al.) as the prediction/probability layer and PASTE's pattern-tuple + opportunistic scheduler (Sui et al.) as the execution layer, with a two-tier idempotent-vs-mutating speculation gate as the one necessary addition for a software-factory context where many Skills have expensive-to-reverse side effects.
- **Confidence:** medium-high. High confidence that the two mechanisms are formally composable (verified by reading both methods sections directly, not abstracts) and that FSM-state-conditioning dominates PASTE's own context-mining by the FSM paper's own baseline table. Medium confidence on the idempotent/mutating gating addition, because neither paper tests a production software-engineering Skill-dispatch setting with genuinely irreversible side effects (PASTE's own benchmarks are deep-research/bug-fix/scientific-research tasks, not full CI/CD-adjacent mutating actions) — this piece is reasoned extension, not evidence-backed replication.
- **Boundary conditions:** This holds for Skill categories with ≥~700 observed traces (the FSM paper's own convergence-bound worked example) and with bounded, small activity alphabets (6–42 symbols in the validated datasets) — i.e., Skill taxonomies on the order of a few dozen per category, not thousands. Outside that regime (cold-start Skill categories, or an unbounded/open vocabulary of ad hoc tool names), the FSM's guarantees don't apply and PASTE's own min-support floor (σ) becomes the only fallback discipline.
- **What would change this position:** A direct head-to-head benchmark showing PASTE's PrefixSpan-mined context actually matches or beats FSM-state-conditioning on next-tool cross-entropy or on end-to-end speculative-hit-rate (neither paper reports this comparison — it's inferred transitively through the FSM paper's RPNI/k-Tails baseline table, not measured on PASTE's own pattern pool). A production case study showing speculative staging of mutating actions caused incidents despite the two-tier gate would also falsify the specific safety-extension piece (but not the core composition claim).
- **Evidence weight:** 2 primary papers, both read in full methods/results sections, structurally compatible with no counter-evidence found; 0 sources tested the actual composition (it is proposed here, not benchmarked in the literature); 2 secondary corpus sources (SOP glossary, agent-skills repo) corroborate that the current production baseline is purely rule-based/deterministic, which is the state this architecture would need to beat to be worth deploying.

## Open questions

- Does PASTE's pattern-mining pipeline, if re-run with FSM-state context instead of PrefixSpan windows, actually measure a speculative-hit-rate or latency improvement over the paper's reported 48.5%/1.8×? No source answers this; it would require an actual experiment, not more reading.
- What is the right cross-entropy/AUROC threshold at which a software factory should flip a Skill category from "deterministic SOP" (per [[what-is-an-ai-agent-sop]]) to "FSM-driven probabilistic dispatch"? Neither corpus source addresses the economics of this transition (engineering cost of building/maintaining the FSM pipeline vs. latency/throughput gained).
- Neither paper tests multi-agent (cross-agent, not just cross-trace) topology — the factory's "5,000-person" framing implies many concurrent agent instances sharing Skill-dispatch infrastructure, which raises contention/isolation questions PASTE's single-agent-serving framing doesn't model.
